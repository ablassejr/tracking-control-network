					\begin{column}{0.495\linewidth}
						\begin{posterpanel}[height=\rowAh]{Abstract}
							A neural network (NN) tracking control for discrete-time nonlinear
							systems with known internal dynamics is designed. The adversarial attack
							on the tracking control NN is modeled as additive stochastic noise
							injected into the output of the signal function of the controller. The
							adversarially perturbed system and the corresponding closed-loop tracking
							dynamics are derived, and the resulting degradation in tracking
							performance is demonstrated. The defense is based on rounding the noisy
							output of the signal function. When the perturbation magnitude is less
							than one half, the rounding recovers the correct signal function value,
							and the defended closed-loop dynamics reduce to the unperturbed system.
							The nominal MATLAB reference and the corresponding C++ implementation
							agree to machine precision. The attacked implementations show the same
							qualitative degradation pattern under independent stochastic samples,
							while the defended C++ response coincides with the nominal reference
							when the perturbation is inside the rounding-recovery threshold. The
							elapsed time of the C++ implementation is less than that of the MATLAB
							implementation by approximately two orders of magnitude.
						\end{posterpanel}
					\end{column}
					\begin{column}{0.495\linewidth}
						\begin{posterpanel}[height=\rowAh]{Introduction}
							The tracking control problem is one of the most fundamental practical
							problems in control theory, where the goal is to design a controller
							that allows a system to follow a desired trajectory or reference signal
							over time. In recent years, neural networks have been increasingly used
							in control systems due to their ability to approximate complex nonlinear
							functions and adapt to changing conditions. However, the use of neural
							networks in control systems also introduces new vulnerabilities,
							particularly to adversarial attacks. Small, practically undetectable
							perturbations in the input of a neural network can lead to outsized
							adverse effects on the output, which can be particularly problematic in
							control systems where safety and reliability are paramount.

							A tracking control neural network (NN) for the known affine in the
							inputs, discrete-time nonlinear system was presented in~[1]. The paper
							describes a method of tracking control that is of moderate complexity
							and requires no training. This method uses a signum activation function
							of the tracking error, as well as a diagonal matrix of the learning rate
							parameters to drive the system state towards the reference trajectory in
							finite time. The method is inherently robust to bounded uncertainties
							and disturbances.
						\end{posterpanel}
					\end{column}
				\end{columns}
			\end{minipage}

			\begin{minipage}[t][\rowBh]{\linewidth}
				\begin{columns}[T,totalwidth=\linewidth]
					\begin{column}{0.328\linewidth}
						\begin{posterpanel}[height=\rowBh]{Mathematical Model and Block Diagram}
							The known affine in the inputs, discrete-time nonlinear system
							obtained by Euler approximation can be presented as follows~[1]:
							\begin{postereqbox}{(1)}
								$x(k{+}1)=x(k)+\tau\bigl[f(x(k))+g(x(k))u(k)\bigr]$
							\end{postereqbox}
							where $x(k)$ is the discrete-time state, $f$ and $g$ are the internal
							and coupling dynamics, $u(k)$ is the control input, $\tau$ is the
							sampling step, and $e(k)=x(k)-r(k)$ is the tracking error with respect
							to the reference $r(k)$. The control input is given by~[1]
							\begin{postereqbox}{(2)}
								$u(k)=g(x(k))^{-1}\bigl[-f(x(k))+\Delta r(k)-\beta\,\operatorname{sgn}(e(k))\bigr]$
							\end{postereqbox}
							where $\beta=\operatorname{diag}(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n)$ is the
							diagonal matrix of learning-rate parameters and
							$\Delta r(k)=r(k{+}1)-r(k)$ is the first-order finite difference of
							the reference. Substituting~(2) into~(1) yields the tracking error
							recursion
							\begin{postereqbox}{(3)}
								$e(k{+}1)=e(k)-\beta\,\operatorname{sgn}(e(k))$
							\end{postereqbox}
							The adversarial attack is modeled~[3] as an additive stochastic
							perturbation $\epsilon(k)$ with normal distribution, zero mean, and
							standard deviation $\sigma=1$, injected at the output of the signal
							function:
							\begin{postereqbox}{(4)}
								$\widetilde{s}(k)=\operatorname{sgn}(e(k))+\epsilon(k)$
							\end{postereqbox}
							Since $\operatorname{sgn}(e(k))\in\{-1,0,+1\}$, the defended output
							obtained by nearest-integer rounding
							\begin{postereqbox}{(5)}
								$\hat{s}(k)=\operatorname{round}(\widetilde{s}(k))$
							\end{postereqbox}
							satisfies $\hat{s}(k)=\operatorname{sgn}(e(k))$ whenever
							$|\epsilon(k)|<\tfrac{1}{2}$, so the defended closed-loop state
							equation reduces to the unperturbed system.

							The defended closed-loop functional block diagram derived from
							equations~(1)--(5) is shown in Fig.~1.

							\begin{center}\includesvg[width=0.90\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{defended-system.svg}\end{center}
							\posterfigcaption{Fig.\,1.}{Functional block diagram of the defended
							closed-loop system. The block $\mathrm{S}$ computes

							disturbance, $\mathrm{R}$ is nearest-integer rounding, $\Delta$
							forms $r(k{+}1)-r(k)$, and LAESS applies the control law using
							$f$, $g$, and $\beta$.}
						\end{posterpanel}
					\end{column}
					\begin{column}{0.328\linewidth}
						\begin{minipage}[t][\rowBh][t]{\linewidth}
							\begin{posterpanel}[height=0.315\rowBh]{Functional Description and Comparative Analysis}
								The controller of Fig.~1 moves the state $x(k)$ toward the
								reference $r(k)$ through the signum activation function and the
								diagonal learning-rate matrix $\beta$, which determine the
								finite-time convergence of $e(k)$ to zero~[1]. Compared with
								the neural-network optimal control approach of~[2], which minimizes
								a quadratic cost functional and requires offline training, this
								tracking controller has moderate complexity and requires no
								training. The defended diagram extends the unperturbed system
								of~[1] by adding the stochastic perturbation $\epsilon(k)$
								after $\mathrm{S}$ and the rounding block $\mathrm{R}$. Compared
								with the projected-gradient defense of~[4], the proposed defense
								uses the discrete codomain $\{-1,0,+1\}$ of the signum activation
								to obtain the recovery condition $|\epsilon(k)|<\tfrac{1}{2}$.
							\end{posterpanel}

							\vspace{\innergap}

							\begin{posterpanel}[height=0.395\rowBh]{Runtime Comparison}

								Simulation implementations of the unperturbed and perturbed
								systems were developed in MATLAB and C++. The elapsed time
								of the full CSTR simulation over $n=45{,}000$ steps with
								time step $\tau=0.001$\,s is shown in Table~1. The results show that
								the C++ implementation is approximately two orders of magnitude
								faster for both configurations. The nominal state and tracking
								error trajectories are identical between the MATLAB and C++
								implementations to machine precision. For the attacked case,
								independent stochastic samples give qualitatively consistent
								but not identical trajectories.

							\vspace{\innergap}

							\begin{posterpanel}[height=0.250\rowBh]{Conclusions}
								A defense based on rounding the noisy output of the signal function
								is formulated for known affine discrete-time nonlinear systems. The
								two main results are: (i)~when the perturbation magnitude satisfies
								$|\epsilon(k)|<\tfrac{1}{2}$, the rounding defense recovers the
								clean signal value, so the defended closed-loop dynamics coincide
								with the unperturbed system; and (ii)~the C++ implementation
								reproduces the nominal MATLAB reference to machine precision while
								substantially reducing elapsed simulation time. The attacked MATLAB
								and C++ trajectories are not expected to be identical
								unless the same stochastic samples are prescribed.
							\end{posterpanel}
						\end{minipage}
					\end{column}
					\begin{column}{0.328\linewidth}
						\begin{posterpanel}[height=\rowBh]{Simulation Results}
							The closed-loop dynamics are simulated on the continuous stirred-tank
							reactor (CSTR) benchmark with $\alpha=1.0$, $\lambda=0.3$,
							$\gamma=20.0$, $B=1.0$, $D=0.072$, $\beta=100$, and sampling step
							$\tau=0.001$\,s, over $n=45{,}000$ iterations. At each time step a
							Gaussian noise with zero mean and standard deviation $\sigma=1$ is
							added to the output of the signal function as in~(4) and
							rejection-sampled to satisfy $|\epsilon(k)|<\tfrac{1}{2}$, so the
							perturbation is bounded by the rounding-recovery threshold on every
							iteration. The defended system is simulated in C++ and
							compared against the unperturbed reference system.
